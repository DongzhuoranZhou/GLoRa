\chapter{Introduction}

Graph learning systems are often expected to predict a target node from information that lies elsewhere in the graph.
When that information is many hops away, the model must carry it through the graph before it can affect the prediction.

Graphs represent entities together with the relations that connect them.
They appear in molecular structures, web links, recommendation systems, road networks, financial transactions, and many other domains where the object of interest is not independent of its context.
A prediction about a target node may depend on nearby nodes, on a wider neighbourhood, or on a source node connected through a path that spans many hops in the graph.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) are a central family of models for learning from this kind of data \cite{scarselli2008graph}.
Most GNNs use message passing.
At each layer, a node aggregates information from its one-hop neighbourhood, combines that information with its current representation, and produces a new node representation for the next layer.
After one layer, a node can use information from its immediate neighbours.
After several layers, it can use information from nodes at larger graph distance.

Repeated one-hop neighbourhood aggregation is one of the main strengths of GNNs.
It lets a model process graphs of different sizes, reuse parameters across the graph, and keep predictions independent of the order in which nodes are stored.
The same design creates the central difficulty studied here.
If the correct prediction for a target node depends on information at large graph distance, that information must be preserved across many message-passing layers before it can affect the final prediction.

Benchmark design makes this difficulty measurable.
A high score on a graph dataset is useful only after the benchmark makes clear what the score certifies.
For a long-range claim, the question is whether the system used information at the intended graph distance or learned an easier shortcut that fits the data.

This thesis studies long-range graph learning as both a modelling problem and an evaluation problem.
The included paper, GLoRa, addresses the evaluation problem through a synthetic benchmark generator for long-range dependency learning in graphs \cite{zhou2025glora}.
For a chosen dependency length, GLoRa generates node-classification examples that are designed so that fitting the examples requires learning the intended dependency.
The paper uses the benchmark to evaluate a broad set of graph learning systems.
Its results show that many systems struggle as the dependency length grows, while the diagnostics in the tested setting do not support over-smoothing, over-squashing, or vanishing gradients as explanations for the observed failure.

The rest of the introduction connects GLoRa to the broader thesis argument: long-range dependencies matter, message-passing systems make dependency length technically difficult, and evaluation must separate intended dependencies from easier alternatives.
It then states the research questions, summarizes the contributions, and outlines the remaining chapters.

\section{Why Graph Learning Matters}

Many prediction problems depend on relations between objects rather than isolated features.
In graph learning, those relations are part of the input rather than background context.
GNNs therefore build node representations from both features and graph structure.
Relevant information can lie beyond the target node's one-hop neighbourhood, so long-range dependency learning is a general requirement rather than a special property of one benchmark.

In a molecule, the behaviour of one atom depends on the structure in which it is embedded.
In a social network, a user's behaviour may depend on nearby users, on communities at larger graph distance, or on chains of influence that pass through several intermediaries.
In a traffic network, congestion at one location may depend on roads and intersections that are not adjacent to it.
In a financial network, suspicious activity can involve a sequence of transactions spread across many accounts.

In all four cases, the input cannot be reduced to a fixed independent vector for each object without losing part of the problem.
The relations between objects carry information, and graph learning methods are designed to use that information directly.

GNNs became influential because they provide a flexible way to learn over such relational data \cite{scarselli2008graph}.
Early and widely used architectures include graph convolutional networks, GraphSAGE, graph attention networks, gated graph neural networks, simplified graph convolution, and graph isomorphism networks \cite{kipf2017semi,hamilton2017graphsage,velickovic2018gat,li2016ggnn,wu2019sgc,xu2019gin}.
They differ in how they aggregate and transform neighbourhood information.
Despite these differences, they share the idea that node representations are built through repeated one-hop neighbourhood aggregation.
This shared design gives them a useful inductive bias for graph-structured data.

These applications also show why the thesis treats long-range graph learning as general rather than benchmark-specific.
Work on social influence and recommendation uses graph structure to model information flow across social and user-item networks \cite{qiu2018deepinf,fan2019graph,liu2022social}.
Urban prediction tasks use graph structure to model traffic and air quality over spatial networks \cite{li2021traffic,iskandaryan2023air}.
Internet safety and finance include tasks such as fake news detection and anti-money laundering, where relevant signals may only appear through multi-step patterns \cite{han2020fake,weber2018aml}.
Biochemical tasks include protein interaction and drug side-effect prediction, where properties can depend on structures that are not local in the graph \cite{jha2022protein,bongini2022drugnn}.

These domains do not all require the same model or the same definition of graph distance.
They do show that learning from graphs often means learning from relations that extend beyond immediate neighbours.
The ability to use information at large graph distance is part of what graph learning systems are expected to do.
Making dependency length an explicit benchmark variable, as in GLoRa, enables that ability to be measured directly.

\section{Long-Range Dependencies in Graphs}

A target prediction has a long-range dependency when its correct label depends on information at large graph distance.
In node classification, the target is a node, and the relevant information may lie several hops away.
The prediction may depend on the source node itself, but it may also depend on the path, pattern, or interaction that connects the source node to the target node.

Graph distance is the basic way to speak about range in this thesis.
If the source information is at graph distance $d$ from the target node, a local message-passing GNN needs at least $d$ layers before that information can affect the target representation through one-hop neighbourhood aggregation.
Being present in the graph is not enough.
The information must be preserved across message-passing layers and used by the learned function.

Long-range dependency learning is not the same as learning on a large graph.
A large graph may have a local target function, while a smaller graph may contain a prediction that depends on a long chain of relations.
The relevant question is which parts of the graph determine the label and how far those parts are from the target node.

The GLoRa paper formalizes one version of this idea through path-aware dependencies \cite{zhou2025glora}.
Chapter 3 gives the formal definition.
The point needed here is that a long-range dependency should make intermediate nodes on a path matter.
If a model can ignore the path and still classify all examples correctly, then the benchmark has not shown that the model learned the intended long-range behaviour.
By varying dependency length explicitly, GLoRa asks whether systems learn this path-aware dependency rather than a local or global shortcut.

Many graph tasks contain correlations that are easier than the dependency they are meant to test.
The label might correlate with node degree, graph size, local feature counts, or another statistic that does not require the learned function to use the intended path information.
A model that uses such a statistic can obtain a high score while failing to learn the dependency of interest.
Long-range dependency learning is also an evaluation problem: the benchmark must make the intended dependency necessary.

\section{Why Long-Range Dependencies Are Difficult}

Message passing makes dependency length an architectural issue.
A natural response is to add message-passing layers, because each layer expands the target node's receptive field by one hop.
In principle, a target node can then receive information from nodes at larger graph distance.
In practice, adding layers changes the learning problem in several ways.

One common explanation is over-smoothing.
Over-smoothing describes the tendency of node representations to become too similar as message passing is repeated many times \cite{li2018deeper}.
If the model maps many nodes to nearly the same representation, it becomes harder to keep the distinctions that matter for the prediction.
This matters for long-range dependency learning because long paths usually require more message-passing steps.

Another common explanation is over-squashing.
Over-squashing describes a bottleneck in which information from many nodes must be compressed into fixed-size representations as it moves through the graph \cite{alon2021bottleneck}.
When a target node can be influenced by many nodes at large graph distance, the number of relevant signals may grow quickly.
If those signals must pass through a narrow part of the graph or a fixed-dimensional vector, some information can be lost.

A third explanation comes from optimization.
Deep neural networks can suffer from vanishing gradients, where the training signal becomes weak as it is propagated backward through many layers \cite{glorot2010difficulty,bengio1994learning}.
For graph models, this problem can interact with the graph structure.
If the layers that first process the source information do not receive useful gradient information, the model may fail to learn how to preserve that information.

These explanations have motivated many architectural changes.
Some methods add residual or dense connections, normalization, dropout on edges, or propagation schemes designed to make deep graph models easier to train.
Other methods rewire the graph, use diffusion, introduce shortest-path information, or use positional encodings.
Graph transformers go further by allowing broader attention patterns between nodes, often together with structural or positional information \cite{dwivedi2022long,kreuzer2021san,rampasek2022gps}.

These architectural directions target different possible causes, so a change in accuracy does not by itself identify which cause is active.
An intervention designed to reduce over-smoothing may improve accuracy without showing that the model learned a longer dependency.
Rewiring can shorten paths, and transformers can allow broader information exchange, but neither change identifies the dependency used by the learned function.
For GLoRa, these explanations are hypotheses to test rather than assumptions that automatically explain any drop in accuracy.

\section{Evaluation as a Central Problem}

Evaluation is central because benchmark accuracy and evidence of dependency length are different claims.
For many purposes, benchmark accuracy is still a reasonable comparison: if a model improves performance on a realistic task, the improvement can be useful even when the exact target function is unknown.
A claim about long-range dependency learning is more specific because it says that the model can learn a dependency at a certain graph distance.

Real-world graph benchmarks are valuable because they connect research to practical tasks.
Their limitation is that the data-generating process is not controlled.
When a system performs well on such a dataset, the result does not identify which dependency the system used.
The model may have learned a genuinely long-range signal, a local proxy, a global statistic, or a mixture of several cues.

This limitation has been noted in the long-range graph benchmark literature.
Benchmarks such as OGB, TUDataset, and LRGB support useful comparisons, but their dependency structure is hard to certify in the sense needed here \cite{hu2020ogb,morris2020tudataset,dwivedi2022long,dwivedi2023benchmarking}.
Some analyses also argue that proposed long-range benchmarks may contain weaker long-range requirements than intended \cite{tonshoff2023gap}.
Such findings do not make these benchmarks useless, but they limit what the scores can establish about dependency length.

Synthetic benchmarks can address this gap because the designer controls the graph structure and the label rule.
They can specify the intended dependency length, generate many examples, and test systems under controlled conditions.
That control makes synthetic benchmarks useful for diagnosis, and it also makes their design consequential.

A synthetic benchmark can fail in two opposite ways.
First, it can allow shortcut functions.
In that case, a system can fit the examples without learning the intended long-range dependency.
Second, it can require a target function that the evaluated model family cannot express.
In that case, failure on the benchmark may show an expressivity mismatch rather than a failure to learn a long-range dependency.

The GLoRa paper argues that several earlier synthetic benchmarks fall into one of these two problems \cite{zhou2025glora}.
Examples such as Synthetic Chains, Graph Transfer, Ring Transfer, Tree-Neighbours, h-Proximity, Colour-Connectivity, Conditional Recall, and Tree-Max were designed to study long-range behaviour \cite{gu2020implicit,digionvanni2023oversquashing,gutteridge2023drew,alon2021bottleneck,abboud2022shortest,rampasek2021hierarchical,lukovnikov2021improving}.
These benchmarks remain useful, but they do not always guarantee that a system must learn a dependency of the intended length.
Benchmark design is therefore part of the scientific problem because architectural results can only be interpreted against the evidence that the benchmark provides.

\section{The Role of GLoRa}

By treating dependency length as a controlled input to a node-classification benchmark, GLoRa tests whether graph learning systems can learn dependencies of a specified length \cite{zhou2025glora}.
For each length parameter, GLoRa generates binary node-classification examples.
Each example contains a target node and paths leading to it.
The intended label depends on whether a source node is connected to the target node through a path with the required feature pattern.

The construction is designed to block simple shortcut solutions.
Negative examples contain a hole on the relevant path.
Additional paths and additional holes are added so that simple counts, local patterns, or global graph statistics do not reveal the label.
The intended long-range dependency should be necessary for fitting the generated examples.

Expressibility is the second requirement.
A benchmark for learning should not ask a model to learn something outside the model class and then interpret failure as a long-range learning failure.
The paper connects this requirement to work on the logical expressiveness of GNNs \cite{barcelo2020logical,grohe2023descriptive}.

The empirical part of GLoRa evaluates several families of systems: standard GNNs, systems designed to mitigate over-smoothing, systems designed to mitigate over-squashing, and graph transformer systems \cite{zhou2025glora}.
The evaluation increases the dependency length and observes how accuracy changes.
Many systems fail at modest dependency lengths, and even the better-performing systems do not give strong evidence of learning the longer dependencies tested in this setting.

The paper then tests three common explanations for the performance drop.
The inspected cases do not show the representation collapse expected from over-smoothing.
The directed construction bounds the number of relevant paths in a way that makes over-squashing an unlikely explanation for the observed failure.
The gradient measurements do not support vanishing gradients as the cause in the tested setting.

The interpretation is deliberately narrow.
Over-smoothing, over-squashing, and vanishing gradients remain relevant to graph learning in general.
The GLoRa experiments show that the specific failure exposed by this benchmark needs a more precise explanation.

At thesis level, GLoRa turns a broad concern into a controlled evaluation question.
Instead of asking only whether long-range graph learning is hard, it asks which dependency length a system can learn when easier alternatives are ruled out by construction.
The benchmark is a diagnostic tool, not only a leaderboard.

\section{Research Questions}

The preceding discussion leaves three unresolved problems.
The first is what makes long-range dependency learning difficult for graph learning systems.
The second is how a benchmark can establish that a system learned the intended dependency rather than a shortcut.
The third is whether the failures observed with GLoRa are explained by the standard accounts of long-range failure.
The research questions address these problems in order.

\begin{description}
\item[RQ 1:]
What makes long-range dependency learning difficult for graph neural networks?
This question connects message passing and graph distance to architectural and optimization limits.
It examines the standard explanations of long-range failure, including over-smoothing, over-squashing, and vanishing gradients.

\item[RQ 2:]
How can a benchmark test whether a graph learning system has learned a dependency of a specified length?
This question asks how benchmark design can rule out shortcut functions while keeping the target function expressible by the evaluated models.
It also asks when performance can be interpreted as evidence about dependency length.

\item[RQ 3:]
What does GLoRa show about current graph learning systems and about the limits of existing explanations?
This question asks how far the evaluated systems can learn under the GLoRa construction and whether the observed degradation is explained by the three common failure modes.
\end{description}

Together, the questions connect the modelling problem, the requirements for valid evaluation, and the diagnosis of empirical failure.

\section{Contributions}

The thesis contribution is anchored in Paper I.
The paper uses controlled node-classification benchmarks to study whether graph learning systems learn long-range dependencies of specified lengths.
It is listed below.

\begin{description}
\item[Paper I]
Dongzhuoran Zhou, Evgeny Kharlamov, and Egor V. Kostylev.
\emph{GLoRa: A Benchmark to Evaluate the Ability to Learn Long-Range Dependencies in Graphs}.
Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2025.
\end{description}

Through this paper and the surrounding thesis discussion, the thesis makes four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item It frames long-range dependency learning in graph neural networks as an evaluation problem as well as a modelling problem.
The thesis argues that high benchmark accuracy is not enough to support a claim about dependency length unless the benchmark rules out easier explanations.

\item It presents GLoRa, a synthetic benchmark generator for controlled long-range dependency evaluation in graphs \cite{zhou2025glora}.
For a chosen length, GLoRa generates node-classification examples designed so that fitting the examples requires learning a path-aware dependency of that length.

\item It connects benchmark design to expressivity.
GLoRa is designed to avoid shortcut functions while keeping the intended function expressible by standard GNN-based systems, which makes poor performance more informative than it would be on an impossible target function \cite{barcelo2020logical,zhou2025glora}.

\item It provides an empirical study of current graph learning systems under the GLoRa benchmark.
The study evaluates standard GNNs, systems aimed at over-smoothing, systems aimed at over-squashing, and graph transformer systems.
It finds that the tested systems struggle as dependency length grows, and it reports diagnostics that do not support over-smoothing, over-squashing, or vanishing gradients as explanations for the observed degradation in the tested setting \cite{zhou2025glora}.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, these contributions are mainly methodological.
GLoRa does not claim to represent every long-range phenomenon in real graphs.
It makes one important question testable under controlled conditions: whether a graph learning system can learn a dependency of a specified graph distance when shortcut solutions are ruled out by construction.

\section{Outline}

The remaining chapters move from graph learning foundations to the GLoRa benchmark and its interpretation.
They first define message passing and long-range dependencies, then review explanations and benchmarks, present GLoRa, and close with limitations and future work.

Chapter 2 introduces graph learning and message-passing graph neural networks.
It defines the basic graph learning tasks used in the thesis and explains how local message passing builds node representations.
It also discusses why expressivity matters when benchmarks are used to interpret model failure.

Chapter 3 focuses on long-range dependencies in graphs.
It explains dependency length, relates it to paths and graph distance, and gives examples from application areas where information at large graph distance can matter.
It also prepares the formal discussion of path-aware dependencies used by GLoRa.

Chapter 4 reviews common explanations of long-range failure.
It discusses over-smoothing, over-squashing, and vanishing gradients as separate but interacting explanations.
It also explains why an accuracy drop with increasing dependency length is not by itself a diagnosis.

Chapter 5 surveys the state of the art in architectures and benchmarks for long-range graph learning.
It covers standard GNNs, deeper GNN variants, graph rewiring methods, graph transformers, and existing benchmark families.
The chapter emphasizes what each benchmark can and cannot show about dependency length.

Chapter 6 presents the GLoRa contribution at thesis level.
It explains the design goals of the generator, the role of positive and negative examples, the reason for adding alternative paths and holes, and the main empirical findings.
It also discusses how the paper's diagnostic experiments should be interpreted.

Chapter 7 concludes the kappa.
It summarizes the thesis, discusses limitations of synthetic benchmark design, and outlines future directions for connecting controlled long-range benchmarks to broader graph learning research.
